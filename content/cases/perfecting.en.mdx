Head of Product and Design, Design Engineer @ Perfecting

# An AI salestech that learned to prove its own value before it grew, and that today trains the reps of the FIESC System.

### Sales practice only pays off if it feels like a real call, and if the manager can show the return to whoever signs the contract.

**Team**\
Four people: the CEO, me on product, a fractional CTO focused on DevOps and someone who runs sales and CS operations

**Clients**\
FIESC System (FIESC, SESI, SENAI, IEL and CIESC), a pilot with RD Station and other B2B companies

**Status**\
In operation, with FIESC committed to buying in the federation's next procurement window

Perfecting is a Brazilian salestech startup. On the platform, reps practise voice calls with AI-simulated buyers before facing the real customer, and managers follow the team's progress. When I joined, in January 2026, there was barely an API. There was no defined price, no way to measure usage and no way to show the client that the practice paid off. I took on discovery, product, design and the entire frontend, and when the CTO left I took on the backend too.

In a four-person startup, the pace is fast and everyone takes on more than their own scope. I also covered CS and marketing, but this case stays with product, data, design and engineering.

#### The problem

There was no shortage of ideas. There was a shortage of evidence of value.

In a small startup, every week in the wrong direction is expensive. And in a B2B product, the person who uses it is not the person who pays. The rep needs to feel that the practice helps, and the manager needs to prove it to finance.

- No reliable reading of where the rep got stuck or gave up.
- No number the manager could take to whoever approves the purchase.
- A price still undefined, in a product where every minute of a call has a language model and infrastructure cost.
- An implementation done by hand, slow and hard to repeat for every new client.

Each of these problems called for a new screen. The temptation was to start designing. What the product needed first was to learn to see itself, and to explain itself to the people who buy it.

#### What I did in the business

### I priced by what the client actually uses.

In January, we ran demos with sales managers, with a conversation before and another after, to understand their willingness to pay. I cross-checked those answers with a competitor benchmark and the real cost of each session, adding up the language model, infrastructure and the margin the team needed to operate. Together with the CEO, we arrived at a model per rep hour, with an implementation fee. The price follows usage, and the client pays for the practice that happens, not for idle licences.

### I shortened the path to the first session.

At first, every implementation was done by hand, and so it was slow. Over time, we learned which materials to ask the client for, which technology to use and how to model the agents from them, and building roleplays stopped being manual. Today implementation takes two weeks and starts with what matters most, understanding the team's gaps before building any roleplay. The sooner the rep practises, the sooner the manager has numbers to defend the purchase.

### I gave the manager the numbers to take to finance.

I designed and coded an ROI calculator that runs inside PerfectingOS, our hub of internal tools, and is used at the end of every proof of concept. It looks at the return from two angles. The first is the manager's time, no longer spent running every roleplay and every review by hand, now covering the whole team. The second is revenue, which grows when the rep learns faster to handle the playbook's objections and shortens the ramp-up (the time until the rep is ready). In an illustrative scenario of 30 reps and 3 managers, the hours saved are the easiest argument to grasp, but what pays for the tool several times over is one extra point of conversion. The assumptions come from the conversations around the January demos, internal data and the benchmark, and they are stated on screen, because a projection only convinces when the client can question it.

#### What I did in the product

### I turned twelve interviews into five problems and one sprint.

I ran twelve interviews with reps and managers and structured the discovery in Figma, holding the team back from jumping to features before the hypotheses were written down. Out came three rep profiles, five macro-problems and 86 solutions, prioritised with MoSCoW and an impact-by-effort matrix.

When prioritising, I reviewed the effort of each solution. Part of the work was frontend and I could do it myself, so several solutions cost less than they seemed and moved up the list. I also looked at the problems together, not one by one. There was no point giving the rep a reason to come back while the call still broke immersion. So the sprint had three fronts in parallel, with the backend on immersion and the frontend on the post-call and onboarding.

### I went into the field at RD Station.

On a research visit with recorded interviews, I found that a rep spent between 10 and 40 minutes preparing each lead, and that this preparation was skipped in some cases. The rep's problem did not start on the call; it started before it. On Perfecting, that context comes ready on the roleplay details screen: who the buyer is, what is being sold and what will be practised.

![Roleplay details: the context before the call](/assets/img/perfecting/briefing.webp)

### I put the rep's competitiveness to work for practice.

The first interviews already made it clear that reps are highly competitive, and that this kind of stimulus was needed for them to come back and practise. So the product gained scores, points and a ranking, and it was great to watch users competing for position among themselves.

The ranking also taught me that the rule matters as much as the screen. It started from the simple average score, and the problem took a while to show. The reps themselves surfaced it, talking to CS through Crisp, the support chat I implemented in the product. In those conversations we understood what was happening: some did a few roleplays, got a high score and stopped, protecting their position. I changed the rule to the average of the three best sessions. That way, practising again never brings anyone down, it can only raise the score, and a bad attempt stops being a risk. It greatly increased the number of sessions, and it reinforced the principle that practice is a safe place to make mistakes.

### I defended realism against the benchmark's features.

In the benchmark of Hyperbound and Yoodli, the references in AI sales roleplay and coaching, the proposal was to bring into the call what competitors showed: live transcription, tips on what to say and a real-time score. I raised that this went against realism. The product's value lies in the pressure of a real conversation, and a screen that feeds the answer pulls the rep out of that state. The proposal was well received and became a product rule. The call keeps only what a real rep would have: the call's goals, checked off as they progress, and what the buyer already knows, like notes on the side that can be collapsed. Score, transcript and feedback come afterwards.

![During the call: only the conversation](/assets/img/perfecting/ligacao.webp)

![After the call: the learning](/assets/img/perfecting/resultado.webp)

### I treated the agent as interface and the client's playbook as the rule.

On a roleplay platform, the simulated buyer is the product's most important screen. I designed the agents' behaviour based on real sales methodologies, wrote the prompts for the voice agents and reviewed the rubrics that generate the feedback. I trained the models on articles by well-known salespeople, so that they assess each call the right way in each methodology. This is part of the implementation. Perfecting's business model is to calibrate the agents to each company's sales process and method. At FIESC, which sells with SPIN Selling, the tracks were built on that method. Today the product works with SPIN, MEDDIC and SPICED.

#### What I did in design

I did all of the product's design, from the rep's screens to the manager's, and took much of it into code myself, as I did with the company website, perfecting.com.br. From the start, I set myself two goals that seemed to pull against each other. The rep needed to feel calm and safe to make mistakes, without feeling watched. And the people who buy needed to see a platform with the trust of an enterprise B2B tool.

Building on the same benchmark, I explored in Figma what made sense to bring into Perfecting. The principles that guide the product came out of it.

- **Realism over didacticism.** The call feels like a real call, with nothing on screen feeding the rep the answer.
- **The rep's day-to-day, simulated.** Practice follows the real routine: getting the lead's context, calling, taking notes and seeing the learnings recorded afterwards, as in a CRM.
- **A safe place to make mistakes.** The practice belongs to the rep. The feedback talks to them about what to improve, and the manager follows progress rather than watching every step.
- **Enterprise trust.** A calm, sober look, colour with meaning and numbers the manager can take to whoever signs the contract.

Before the screens, I drew the rep's and the manager's flows, from login to the session result.

![The rep's flow](/assets/img/perfecting/fluxo-vendedor.webp)

On the Figma canvas, I explored the alternatives side by side. One example is choosing what to practise before the call, by methodology letter or by playbook moment, and how each choice shows up during the call.

![Explorations of the practice choice and the call](/assets/img/perfecting/figma-exploracoes.webp)

What was approved became a design system. The tokens were born in Figma and are the same in the code. I organised everything in Storybook in four layers: foundations (colour, typography, spacing, radii, borders and elevation), patterns, components and screens, in 284 stories, each pointing to the full rule in the design guide. The product has a look of its own, with the brand indigo as the main colour, amber as the accent and terracotta in the framing, and every token says where it belongs. The indigo, for example, never fills large blocks: it appears only on the CTA, the active state, focus and the highlighted number.

### I designed the dashboard the manager opens in the morning.

Managers have no time to explore dashboards. The home screen answers in seconds whether the team is practising, with four indicators (sessions, average score, practice time and active reps) over seven- and thirty-day windows. Next to them, the team ranking. Below, a map of objections handled per rep shows where each one still slips. For newcomers, a four-step walkthrough goes from adding the team to analysing the metrics. It is the same set of numbers the calculator promises, now actually measured.

### I made a minute and fifteen seconds of waiting disappear.

After each call, the feedback takes on average a minute and fifteen seconds to process. That is too long for a spinner. I redesigned the wait so it became part of the post-call, showing each part of the analysis as it is ready while the scorecard assembles, and asking the rep to rate the call in the meantime. In practice, reps stopped noticing the wait, without the backend having to get any faster.

![The wait after the call](/assets/img/perfecting/loading.webp)

### I reorganised practice around the way the sale happens.

The roleplay list, which was grouped by offer, is now grouped by playbook moment and call type, because in consultative selling the offer often does not even exist yet at the diagnosis stage.

![Roleplays grouped by playbook moment](/assets/img/perfecting/roleplays.webp)

#### What I did in engineering

In the code, I built the entire frontend, took on the backend when the CTO left and now share it with the fractional CTO. I also built the internal tools and set up the AI operations behind the roleplays.

### I built Facility, Perfecting's roleplay generator.

Building a roleplay meant filling in field after field, for every client. Facility is an internal piece of software I built end to end, based on feedback from clients and the team and on ideas I had to speed up our processes. In express creation, the team pastes a text or uploads a client file, and the AI structures the offer, the audience profile, the objections, the scenario and the rubrics. With one click, the roleplay goes to the right account, where Perfecting's AI generates the agents, the company, the dialogues, the criteria and the voice. Around that, Facility organises tracks, playbooks, the roleplay library and calibration, each client's guidelines and a readiness area for assessment. It was one of the best-received tools inside the company, and part of what brought implementation down to two weeks.

![Facility, the roleplay generator](/assets/img/perfecting/facility.webp)

### I set up AI Ops for the roleplays, with evals and fine-tuning.

Every change to one agent can make another worse. I set up an evals process for the roleplays in which autonomous agents simulate the calls, a blind judge compares the new version with the previous one, and nothing goes to production without passing. For fine-tuning, I built a synthetic data pipeline in Python, with conversations generated between agents and a quality-scoring step.

#### What I did with the data

### I instrumented the whole product in PostHog.

I designed and implemented the tracking plan in PostHog, from sign-up to the end of each roleplay, with attributes for plan, organisation, role and team size. That let us compare rep with manager and company with company, instead of looking at an average that represented no one. That plan produced the metrics that now support the conversation with the client.

### I watched the session recordings, and fixed what they showed.

The session recordings showed what no number did. I saw users clicking on dead areas of the screen and bugs that only appeared at certain moments of the journey, and I often fixed them straight in the code, with the fix live almost immediately. The most important finding was at the end of the session. Reps reached the result and often did not know what to do next, so I changed the copy to show them the next steps. Another finding came from clicks on the roleplay cards: almost nobody clicked "Practise now". I swapped the button for "View details", which goes deeper into the roleplay before the rep takes action and gives them the context of the call.

#### What I did with clients

### I learned from a contract that did not come.

RD Station was our pilot, and the reps' feedback was so positive that the contract had already been sent to legal. What stopped the purchase was a business decision on the client's side. A company RD had acquired also launched roleplays, and it already had call transcription inside the reps' workflow. Doing a roleplay there became just one more step in what the team already did. The loss did not invalidate the product's thesis, since the reps approved of the practice. It showed that, in B2B, a well-rated pilot and a contract with legal are still not revenue, and that the workflow the client already lives in weighs as much as the product.

### I audited the product with autonomous agents before putting the FIESC System on it.

Before releasing the platform to the federation's reps, I ran an audit in staging with autonomous agents. An AI rep called the AI buyer, end to end, in a lab without voice, in a 22-step simulation. The result was harsh. In 6 of the 22 calls the feedback never arrived. The live checklist took 10.5 seconds at p90, against a target of 3. A third of the checks were triggered by the buyer's speech. In total, 23 critical and 97 serious problems.

I closed the audit with a final report for the team, and each finding became a measurable acceptance criterion. From it came an execution plan in waves, by owner and by risk, which I put together with the fractional CTO, who at that point also took on backend because of the workload. The launch came to depend on numbers, not impressions. Switching the language model brought latency within target, and the product reached the reps ready to be used every day.

### I put the product under load before launch.

An audit shows whether each call works on its own. It does not show what happens when a whole team practises at the same time, which is exactly what a launch at a federation causes. Testing that beforehand was my idea. I had more than 20 agents calling simultaneously to find out how many calls the product could handle before the experience degraded. It was better to find that limit in staging than on FIESC's first day.

### Every week I put together a report that proves the value of the practice.

Every week, I prepare a report for managers from the practice sessions, comparing the team's usage and progress with the previous week. It also brings recurrence, a reading by team, the bottleneck of the period and a focus plan for the next cycle. Managers do not need to open any dashboard to know whether the team improved, and it is with that data that they defend the purchase.

#### Today

### The FIESC System's reps practise on Perfecting, from the federation to SESI, SENAI, IEL and CIESC.

Every week, the report closes the loop. The rep sees their own progress, the manager follows the team just by reading the report, and Perfecting proves the platform's value with real usage.

In every follow-up meeting, managers come back to two points. The first is how real the conversation feels. The second is being able to test questions and approaches with an agent before using them with a client, without burning a single lead. The federation has committed to acquiring the tool in its next procurement window, and the revenue business case is being closed with them.

The product is also starting to grow beyond the screen. Perfecting On Field is a new product that complements the manager's journey, bringing the same visibility to people who sell in person, with remote coaching.

The screens were never the main deliverable. The deliverable was a product that can be measured, a price that follows usage and a set of principles clear enough that anyone on the team can decide without asking me.

#### In hindsight

### When I arrived, almost nothing existed, and the natural urge was to build one screen after another. Stopping to define what to measure before there was anything to measure felt slow. Looking at it now, those were the most important weeks of the project. Without them, every new screen would be just another well-drawn opinion.

### Pricing per rep hour tied the price to usage. That changed what we needed to measure, how implementation had to work and what the manager needed to show finance. Pricing became a product decision.

### The RD pilot taught me that good feedback does not guarantee a contract. In B2B, the workflow the client already lives in weighs as much as the product, and no pilot is revenue before the signature.

### At Perfecting, my medium stopped being just Figma and FigJam. It became the cost-per-session spreadsheet, PostHog, the agent prompt, the evals, the repository and the many meetings with users and with the people who pay, bringing ready-made reports that show the team's performance. That path, from design to the numbers, is where I work today.
