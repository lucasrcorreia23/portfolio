# A port gate that relied on paper and in-person checks now welcomes the visitor before they even arrive.

### The visit starts days earlier, on the phone, and ends at a kiosk that prints the access sticker.

**My role**\
Product Designer at Atomsix. Discovery, flows, portal, communications and kiosk.

Portonave is a container terminal in Navegantes, Santa Catarina. Every day, drivers, service providers, business visitors and groups pass through its gate. The project's question was simple: who enters the terminal, and with what authorisation?

![The self-service kiosk in the storyboard and on screen](/assets/img/portonave/totem-checkin.webp)

---

#### If you read only one part

## The gate relied on manual steps and in-person checks. At peak times, that meant queues, rework and little control over who was inside.

## What we did was move the gate to before arrival: booking, documents and guidance handled on the phone, and a kiosk for check-in.

## The result is a journey designed for four profiles, with a scheduling portal, a sequence of messages leading up to the visit day and a kiosk that prints the access sticker.

---

#### The problem

The queue at the gate started long before the gate.

In the project's own words, the gate "relies on manual steps and in-person checks that slow the process down, especially at times of high demand". The journey map showed where that slowness came from:

- Little signage for pedestrians and vehicles.
- Paper, badges and forms filled in on the spot.
- Drivers who do not know the layout of the terminal.
- Mismatched communication between the haulier and the terminal.
- Business visitors with little tolerance for delay, who expect a clean, corporate experience.

![The problem, in the project's words](/assets/img/portonave/problema.webp)

The temptation was to design a faster reception system. What the gate needed first was to stop depending on the moment of arrival.

#### What I did in discovery

### I mapped the arrival journey, not just the reception.

The journey map ran from booking to access, through the wait, the arrival and the registration. At each stage I captured the actions, goals, pain points and opportunities. The strongest pain points were not at the counter: they were on the way to it.

![Gate journey map](/assets/img/portonave/journey-map.webp)

### I designed for four profiles with different stories.

The leader of a visitor group wants to book everyone at once and know who still has something outstanding. The business visitor wants simple pre-registration and a quick way in. The service provider without a booking needs to be identified and approved by the internal host. And the employee who forgot their badge needs a temporary sticker without the bureaucracy. Each profile became a line-drawn storyboard, told from the person's point of view.

![The group leader sharing the visit](/assets/img/portonave/fluxo-grupo.webp)

### I listed what could be taken out of the gate.

The opportunities came out of the stories: filling in data automatically, a shareable link for groups, a distinct welcome for those who had already booked, automatic access by facial recognition, QR code or turnstile, and compliance in collecting third-party data.

---

#### What I did in the product

### I put booking in a portal with guest approval.

The internal host sees visits in a list with a calendar and creates a booking with vehicle, reason and host, which becomes an Outlook event straight away. Additional visitors go through approval, and those marked as VIP get in without needing the identification sticker.

![Scheduling portal with VIP approval](/assets/img/portonave/portal-vip.webp)

### I made the visit start 48 hours earlier.

I designed a sequence of messages leading up to the visit day. Two days before, the person receives their status. One day before, the document checklist for their profile. On the morning of the visit, anything still outstanding. Two to three hours before, the route on the map, the car park and a reminder about facial recognition. After the visit, a satisfaction survey. The messages arrive by WhatsApp and email.

![Visit-day guidance on WhatsApp](/assets/img/portonave/whatsapp.webp)

### I replaced the counter with a self-service kiosk.

The visitor identifies themselves by CPF or QR code. The kiosk finds the booking, shows the host and the time, and completes the check-in, which notifies the host. At the end, it prints an access sticker with a QR code. Anyone arriving without a registration gets the link on their phone. Anyone registered but without a booking is sent to the gate. The buttons are large pills, and each screen has a single task.

![Kiosk sequence: idle, identified booking and check-in](/assets/img/portonave/totem-sequencia.webp)

### I made the sticker the visit's credential.

The sticker carries the name, the host and a QR code. Once it is on, the service provider is escorted to the right place inside the port, together with the internal host. It is what identifies the visitor while they move around the terminal.

![The visitor sticker in use](/assets/img/portonave/adesivo.webp)

---

#### What changed

### The gate stopped starting at the counter.

What used to be sorted out standing up, at the moment of arrival, is now sorted out beforehand: booking, documents, route and guidance. On the day, the kiosk completes the check-in and hands over the credential. The gate team is left with what only a person can resolve: the cases without a booking and the exceptions.

---

#### In hindsight

### The line-drawn storyboards did the most to align the client. Before any screen, everyone could see themselves in the story of one of the four profiles.

### If I did it again, I would test the kiosk at the terminal earlier. It is in the real environment, with sun, noise and hurry, that a self-service screen shows whether it works.
